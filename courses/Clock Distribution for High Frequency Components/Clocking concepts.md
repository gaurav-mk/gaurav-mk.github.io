## Fundamental frequency
In signal processing and electronics, the **fundamental frequency** is the lowest, primary frequency of a repeating periodic waveform. It dictates the basic rate at which the entire signal completes one full cycle.

## Noise Floor
### The Measurement: negative 155.2 dBc per Hz

This is the measurement of the noise floor itself.

- **dBc**: This abbreviation stands for decibels relative to the carrier. If your main 983.04 MHz clock signal is towering at a certain power level, the background noise is 155.2 decibels below that peak. That is a staggering difference. In linear terms, the noise power is roughly a quadrillion times weaker than the signal power.
- **per Hz**: Noise is spread out continuously across the entire frequency spectrum. To make fair and accurate comparisons between different hardware components, engineers measure the noise power inside a tiny, standardized window that is exactly 1 Hertz wide.

# SYSREF Operation
## Step 1: Physical PCB Trace Skew

High speed data converters working above one gigahertz require identical sampling moments across all channels. However, electrical signals travel through printed circuit board copper traces at approximately 150 picoseconds per inch.

When a master clock source sends sampling clocks across unequal trace lengths, relative propagation delays occur. This creates clock skew between converter chips, causing them to sample analog waveforms at offset time instances and destroying phase alignment across channels.

## Step 2: Subclass 1 Clock Distribution Architecture

To solve clock skew and internal divider randomness, the [JESD204 Standard Specification](https://www.google.com/search?q=https://www.jedec.org/standards-documents/focus-outer-areas/memory-configurations-and-modules/jesd204) defines the Subclass 1 clocking architecture.

The system uses a specialized master clock generator IC that produces pairs of differential signals for each target chip. Each pair consists of a continuous high speed Device Clock and a targeted SYSREF signal. PCB designers strictly match the trace lengths between the Device Clock line and the SYSREF line within each individual pair.

## Step 3: Setup and Hold Time Capture Window

SYSREF functions as a precise timing marker. For the receiving converter chip to register SYSREF correctly, the pulse transition must arrive within a strict setup time and hold time window relative to the rising edge of the high speed Device Clock.

At multi gigahertz clock rates, this valid capture window is narrower than 200 picoseconds. Arriving cleanly inside this window prevents metastability and ensures the internal digital logic captures SYSREF on an exact clock cycle.

## Step 4: Resetting the Local Multi Frame Clock Counters

Inside each data converter chip, digital dividers step down the high speed Device Clock to form the Local Multi Frame Clock (LMFC). The LMFC dictates frame packaging and internal processing boundaries.

When the converter captures a valid SYSREF pulse on a Device Clock rising edge, internal control logic forces the LMFC counter back to count zero. This reset occurs simultaneously across all converter chips in the system, forcing their internal frame dividers into identical phase alignment.

## Step 5: SYSREF Operational Modes

Engineers select from three main SYSREF modes based on system noise sensitivity:

- **Continuous Mode:** Pulses repeat continuously at every LMFC boundary. While easy to monitor, continuous transitions risk coupling clock noise into sensitive analog front ends.
    
- **Periodic Gated Mode:** Transmits a controlled burst of pulses during initialization to align dividers, then shuts off.
    
- **One Shot Mode:** Emits a single pulse during initial setup. Once captured, the generator powers down the SYSREF driver entirely, maintaining complete signal quiet during active data conversion.

## Step 6: End to End Deterministic Latency

With internal LMFC counters aligned, data serializers in each converter transmit frame boundaries on the exact same clock cycle.

As serialized data travels across PCB lanes toward the receiving FPGA, small trace length differences cause lanes to arrive with slight offsets. The FPGA routes each lane into an elastic FIFO buffer. The FPGA logic releases data from all buffers simultaneously on a local frame clock edge, completing end to end deterministic latency across every channel in the system.
