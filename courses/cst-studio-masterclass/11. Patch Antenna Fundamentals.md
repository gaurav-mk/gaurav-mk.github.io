> [!abstract] Key idea
> A patch antenna is a **resonant cavity**: fields bounce between the patch and the ground plane, and the **fringing fields** at its edges radiate. The feed must be **matched to 50 Ω** to deliver power well, and an inset feed is a simple way to do that.

## 0.1 Why Patch Antennas
- They are **low profile, light and easy to make**.
- They're used in satellite links, mobile devices, GPS, WLAN, RFID, IoT and biomedical devices.
- The shape sets the resonant frequency, impedance and radiation:

| Shape | Why use it |
|---|---|
| Rectangular, square | Simple to analyze and build |
| Circular | Symmetric patterns, often used for circular polarization |
| Elliptical, triangular, hexagonal, ring, slotted, U-shaped | Smaller size, more bandwidth or multi-band operation |

- The patch acts as a **resonant cavity**. Waves reflect between the patch and ground and form standing waves.
- At resonance, the patch length is about **half a wavelength in the substrate**.
- **Fringing fields** at the edges are what radiate the energy.

## 0.4 Impedance Matching
- **Input impedance** is the ratio of voltage to current at the feed point.
- The antenna must match the line, which is typically **50 Ω**. A mismatch reflects power, which S11 shows.
- **Ways to match:** use an inset feed, move the coaxial probe, or adjust the microstrip line. Then tune while watching S11.

> [!info] Why an inset works
> At the patch edge, voltage is high and current is low, so impedance is high: about 195 Ω here. It falls towards the center, so moving the feed point inwards brings it close to 50 Ω. The ideal depth here is about 9.8 mm, and this design uses 9 mm.

## 0.6 Cutting Insets with Vacuum
- A brick made of **Vacuum** can cut a shape directly. When it overlaps the patch, CST asks what to do, so choose to cut away the highlighted part.
- Subtract gives the same result, as in [[2. Antenna Shape Design|Section 2]].
- Finally, join the patch and the line with Add.
