> [!abstract] Key idea
> A loop, a small slot and a second feed on the back are meant to give **circular polarization** from two ports. The three build steps are a good lesson in what each change does. But the lecture never drives the ports together or plots **axial ratio**, so circular polarization is claimed and not shown.

- In **linear** polarization, the electric field swings back and forth along one line.
- In **circular** polarization, the field keeps its size and its tip turns in a circle.
- It takes **two orthogonal fields** of equal size, 90° apart in phase.
- **Axial ratio** measures the result: the long axis of the field's ellipse over the short axis.
- The benefit is that the receiver's orientation matters far less, which suits satellite and mobile links.

- It's printed on 1 mm FR-4, only 42 × 42 mm, and simulated from 1.5 to 4 GHz. That's about 0.3 λ across at 2.18 GHz.
- **Front:** a square loop with 6 mm wide sides, with a 0.1 mm slit through the bottom side.
- **Back:** a T-shaped strip whose stem runs under the loop's top side, which acts as its ground.
- Port 1 is a discrete port across the slit, and Port 2 is a waveguide port at the top of the T.
- The 1.4 mm stem works out to about 60 Ω on this board, not 50 Ω.

- **Design 1:** this is the same loop as [[27. Rectangular Loop Antenna|Section 27]]. Its mean perimeter is 144 mm, so the rule from [[30. Rectangular Wire Loop Antenna|Section 30]] gives 2.26 GHz, and the dip is at 2.30 GHz. -7.7 dB corresponds to about 120 Ω, the familiar mismatch of a one-wavelength loop.
- **Design 2:** an 11 × 2 mm slot in the bottom side makes current flow around it, forming a small second loop. The first dip moves down to 2.18 GHz, and the match improves past -10 dB. A second dip also appears at 3.58 GHz.
- **Design 3:** the T and Port 2 are added. Past the loop, stem and half the bar make a path of 15 mm, about a quarter wavelength near 3.1 GHz on this board. That fits the dip at Port 2.

> [!tip] Delete and re-add the port
> Cutting the slot removes the faces that Port 1 was attached to, so the old port is deleted and a new one is placed. After any Boolean cut near a port, check the port is still in the right place.

> [!warning] Circular polarization is never tested
> CST ran each port on its own, which is why the far fields are labeled [1] and [2]. A single port doesn't make a circular field. Proving the claim needs **both ports driven together**, 90° apart, and an **axial ratio** plot under 3 dB. The lecture shows neither.

> [!warning] The two ports may not share a band
> The lecture says the frequency shifted, but it's comparing Design 3's **S22** with Design 2's **S11**, which are different ports. Design 3's S11 and S21 exist in the results tree but aren't shown. In Design 2, S11 sits near -5 dB around 3 GHz, exactly where Port 2 is matched. If the T doesn't change that, the two ports have no shared band, and circular polarization needs one.

> [!tip] How to test it
> Plot S11, S22 and S21 together, and find a band where both ports are matched and S21 is low. Then use simultaneous excitation, or combine the two port results, with Port 2 at a 90° phase. Finally, show the far field as axial ratio.

> [!note] Dual band or wideband?
> The course title says dual band, and the intro and slide say wideband. Design 2 has two bands, while Port 2 in Design 3 has one wide band. Neither label is confirmed for the finished two-port antenna.
