> [!abstract] Key idea
> A loop's behavior depends on its **size**. A small loop acts like a magnetic dipole, while a one-wavelength loop is a resonant radiator with its beam along the loop's axis. This design stops at -7 dB because such a loop is about **130 Ω** at resonance, so changing its length can't fix the match.

| | Small loop | Large loop |
|---|---|---|
| Perimeter | Well under λ/10 | About λ |
| Current | Nearly uniform | Reverses around the loop |
| Acts like | A magnetic dipole | A resonant radiator |
| Strongest radiation | In the plane of the loop | Along the loop's axis |
| As a transmitter | Inefficient | Efficient |
| Typical use | Receiving: AM, RFID | VHF, UHF and microwave |

- On the large loop, the top and bottom sides carry current the **same way**, so they act like two dipoles side by side. That's why the beam runs along the axis.
- A **slot loop** is the planar version: a loop cut as a slot in a ground plane.
- A loop can be fed by coax, a microstrip line or a discrete port, and a ground or reflector can shape the pattern.
- Uses include RFID tags and readers, magnetic sensors, wireless power transfer, direction finding, VHF and UHF links, and biomedical telemetry.

> [!warning] Two slips in the narration
> The narration says small loops radiate perpendicular to their plane, but the slide is right: they have a **null along the axis**. It also calls small loops efficient transmitters, when they're **inefficient**: at a perimeter of λ/10, the radiation resistance is only about 0.02 Ω.

> [!info] Is a loop really "magnetic"?
> A small loop is the magnetic twin of a short dipole, so it senses magnetic fields well up close. In the far field, though, every antenna radiates E and H together, so a loop doesn't radiate through the magnetic field alone.

- It uses the Wire template, with a PEC ring made by subtracting a smaller brick from a larger one.

> [!tip] Why 29 mm and not 25 mm
> A loop's reactance reaches zero a little **above** one wavelength, at around 1.1 λ. Here the mean perimeter is 1.14 λ at the dip.

- The 1D plot is a figure of eight, but it's **turned 90°** from a small loop's, with its lobes along the axis.
- 3.39 dBi is about 1.2 dB above a half-wave dipole, because the two sides act as a small array.

> [!warning] Why S11 stops at -7 dB
> -6.95 dB corresponds to about **130 Ω** at resonance, which is normal for a one-wavelength loop. A 50 Ω port can't do better, however the length is tuned. The fix is matching, such as a quarter-wave section of about 81 Ω. Setting the port to about 130 Ω would also show the true match.

> [!warning] "Short range only" doesn't follow
> The lecture says about 70% of the power gets through, so the antenna suits only near-field links and RFID. At -7 dB it's really **80%**, and CST's total efficiency agrees at 79.5%. That's a loss of 1 dB, which barely changes range. Near-field use also calls for an electrically small loop, and this one is a full wavelength around.
