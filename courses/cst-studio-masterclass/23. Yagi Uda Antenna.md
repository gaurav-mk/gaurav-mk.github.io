> [!abstract] Key idea
> A **Yagi-Uda** antenna feeds only one element. The others are excited by coupling, and their lengths and spacing steer the energy into one strong forward beam. They only do that well if they're the right length **at the real wavelength**, so tuning a Yagi takes more care than moving the S11 dip.

- It was invented in **1926** by Hidetsugu Yagi and Shintaro Uda, and it's still common because it's simple, cheap and directional.
- It's a **parasitic array**: only one element is fed, and the rest are driven by mutual coupling.
- **Driven element:** usually a dipole, plain or folded, connected to the feed.
- **Reflector:** slightly longer, placed behind the driven element.
- **Directors:** slightly shorter, placed in front of it.
- **Boom:** the support that holds all the elements in line.

- The driven element radiates, and the nearby elements pick up that energy and re-radiate it.
- An element's **length sets the phase** of its re-radiated wave. A longer one turns energy back towards the front, and shorter ones pass it along forward.
- The waves then add in front and cancel behind, which gives one strong beam along the boom.
- More directors mean more gain and a narrower beam, but a longer antenna and usually less bandwidth.

> [!info] Gain follows boom length
> Published NBS design tables give about 7 dB over a dipole for a 0.4 λ boom, 10 dB at 1.2 λ, and 14 dB at 4.2 λ. Each extra director adds less than the one before.

## 0.3 Design Guidelines
| Part | Typical | This design |
|---|---|---|
| Driven element | About 0.5 λ | 0.5 λ |
| Reflector | 0.5 to 0.55 λ | 0.55 λ |
| Directors | About 0.45 λ | 0.45, 0.40, 0.35, 0.35 λ |
| Spacing | About 0.15 λ, from 0.1 to 0.3 λ | 0.35 λ to the reflector, 0.125 λ to the directors |

- These are starting values, tuned later for impedance and gain.
- **Conventional** Yagis use metal rods. **Printed** ones use copper strips on a PCB. **Quasi-Yagis** add a microstrip feed and balun, which suits wideband designs.
- Uses include TV reception, WLAN, point-to-point links, 5G, satellite and IoT.

> [!warning] The spec slide and the model differ
> The spec slide lists **0.2 λ between directors**, but the parameter list has no such value, and the model uses 0.125 λ for every director gap. The 0.35 λ reflector spacing is also outside the 0.1 to 0.3 λ guideline.

## 0.4 The 6 Element Model
- It targets **2.45 GHz**, where λ = 122.36 mm, using the Wire template from 1 to 6 GHz.
- The elements are 2 mm PEC rods in free space, along Z, with the array running along X.
- Every length is written as a fraction times `lambda`, so one parameter scales the whole antenna.
- A 4 mm gap in the dipole holds a 50 Ω discrete port.
- The dip is -29.67 dB at **2.10 GHz**. That's 14% below the target, so it's not close.
- The 61.18 mm dipole behaves much like the 62.46 mm rod in [[19. Dipole Antenna|Section 19]], which dipped at 2.13 GHz.

> [!warning] Gain hides the mismatch
> At 2.45 GHz this model shows 8.91 dBi gain, but its total efficiency is **-4.54 dB**, so 65% of the power is reflected. The realized gain is only **4.35 dBi**. See [[4. Equation Based Antenna Design|Section 4]] for how the efficiencies differ.

> [!note] Gain above directivity
> Gain reads 8.911 dBi and directivity 8.889 dBi, with radiation efficiency of +0.02 dB. PEC has no loss, so that tiny excess is only numerical noise.

> [!warning] lambda is now only a scale factor
> The real wavelength at 2.45 GHz is still 122.36 mm. With `lambda` = 102.5, every part is **16% shorter than designed**: the dipole is 0.42 λ, the reflector 0.46 λ, and the last directors 0.29 λ. So the guideline lengths no longer hold, and the reflector is below a half wavelength.

> [!tip] A better fix to try
> The driven element mostly sets the match, and the other elements mostly set the pattern. So try shortening **only the dipole**, to about 52.5 mm, and keeping the others at their real design lengths. The lecture doesn't test this.

## 0.6 Ten Elements and a Boom
- Four more directors, the same length as director 4, are added with one **Translate** copy, using a repetition factor of 4.
- The boom is a 2 mm square bar of a new dielectric with εr = 2.1, running a little past both ends.
- **Boolean Insert** fits it around the elements, and a small cut keeps the port gap clear.

| Model | Dip | Gain at 2.45 GHz | Total efficiency | Realized gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 elements, `lambda` = 122.36 | 2.10 GHz | 8.91 dBi | 35% | 4.35 dBi |
| 10 elements, `lambda` = 102.5 | 2.45 GHz | 9.93 dBi | 99% | 9.91 dBi |

- With the boom, S11 is -24.34 dB at 2.453 GHz, and below -10 dB from about 2.3 to 2.6 GHz.

> [!warning] Two changes at once
> The lecture credits the extra directors for the rise from 8.9 to 9.93 dBi, but `lambda` changed between those runs too. A fair test needs the 6 element model at `lambda` = 102.5, which isn't shown. The 1.26 λ boom could also reach about 12 dBi by the NBS tables, so this design has room to improve.

> [!info] The boom result isn't general
> A thin, low-permittivity bar barely changes anything, as seen here. A **metal** boom does change the elements' electrical length, so real designs must correct for it.
