> [!abstract] Key idea
> This antenna pairs a **dipole** on the front with a **loop** on the back, coupled through a thin board, plus a strip acting as a director. The pair gives a wide, one-sided beam from a compact board. The slide claims 0.7 to 1.2 GHz, but the simulation shows **two separate bands**, not one wide band.

- The board is 120 × 120 mm of FR-4, only 0.8 mm thick, simulated from 0.6 to 1.2 GHz.
- **Front:** a dipole with its arms bent into a U around the board's bottom and sides, which is what keeps it compact.
- **Back:** a rectangular loop, and a strip across the top as the director.
- A 50 Ω discrete port feeds the dipole across the 2.4 mm gap.
- The slide describes a half-wavelength dipole and a one-wavelength loop. The sizes in the diagram roughly fit that.

> [!note] Director or reflector?
> The table calls the strip a director, but calls gap d the "Gap between Loop and Reflector". The beam leans towards the strip, so **director** is the right name.

- The loop has no port. It's fed by **coupling** from the dipole.
- Its bottom strip overlaps the dipole by 2.4 mm through the 0.8 mm board, which makes a parallel-plate capacitor of about **3 to 4 pF per arm**.
- That's only about 40 to 50 Ω of reactance at 1 GHz, so the link is strong.
- The notch removes some overlap on one arm, so it can tune the coupling. The lecture doesn't sweep it, so its effect isn't shown.

- A dipole is an electric source and a small loop is a magnetic one, and their fields have different signs on opposite sides.
- Added together, they reinforce on one side and cancel on the other, which gives a one-sided **cardioid**.
- This pairing is known as a **Huygens source**, and it gives a one-sided beam without a large ground plane.
- It's the ideal case. This loop is far from electrically small, so treat it as the idea behind the design and not an exact model.

> [!warning] Wide, not highly directive
> The lecture calls the 0.9 GHz pattern highly directive, but it's **187° wide** at 4.85 dBi. That's a useful wide, one-sided coverage, which is what the slide's word "unidirectional" means. It also calls the 0.6 GHz pattern a doughnut, but that one is out of band, with most of the power reflected.

> [!tip] Look at the dips
> The far field monitors were at 0.6, 0.9 and 1.2 GHz, and **none of them is in a matched band**. So the lecture never shows which element sets which dip. Add monitors at 0.77 and 1.13 GHz, then compare the surface current on the dipole and the loop.
