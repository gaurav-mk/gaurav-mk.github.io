> [!abstract] Key idea
> A **bandpass FSS** does the opposite of a bandstop one: it passes one band and blocks the frequencies on either side. The workflow stays the same as for [[5. Bandstop FSS Design|the bandstop FSS]]: design the unit cell, sweep it, then build the sheet.

## 0.1 Bandstop vs Bandpass
| | Bandstop (Section 5) | Bandpass (this section) |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Block the X band, 8 to 12 GHz | Pass part of the C band, 4 to 8 GHz |
| **Element** | Convoluted square loop | Square loop around a square patch |
| **Copper** | One face | **Both faces** |
| **Substrate** | RT/duroid 5880, 0.127 mm | FR-4, 1 mm |
| **Cell size P** | 6.8 mm, about 0.23 λ | 32 mm, about 0.7 λ |
| **Key result** | S21 about -60 dB near 10 GHz | S21 = -0.21 dB near 6.6 GHz |

- In the passband, S11 is low and S21 is close to 0 dB, so nearly everything passes: **S11 = -31.3 dB, S21 = -0.21 dB**.
- On both sides, S21 falls and S11 rises. Those are the **stop bands**.

> [!info] How metal can pass a band
> Each metal element blocks a band around its own resonance. With a loop and a patch together, a passband can open up between the stop bands. So a single metal loop is bandstop, but several resonators can be bandpass.

> [!tip] Where the power goes
> At the passband, 95.3% of the power passes and 0.07% reflects. The missing 4.6% is absorbed by the lossy materials.
