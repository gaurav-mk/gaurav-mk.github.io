> [!abstract] Key idea
> A **protocol** is the shared language that turns raw electrical signals into meaningful data. Choosing one is a trade-off between **speed, wires, distance, noise immunity, power and cost**. The options run from on-board serial buses (UART, SPI, I2C) to rugged networks (CAN, Ethernet), PC links (USB) and wireless (BLE, Wi-Fi, NFC, Zigbee).

## 0.1 What a Protocol Is
- **Physical interfaces** decide how boards connect:
  - **PMOD**, Digilent's open standard with 6, 8 or 12-pin connectors
  - **Grove**, Seeed Studio's 4-pin plug-and-play system
  - **Arduino headers** for add-on "shields".
- The **Renesas QuickConnect** kits carry all three, so a whole ecosystem of add-on boards plugs straight in.
- A GPIO can only say ON or OFF. Sending **multi-bit data**, such as 28.5 °C or a **14-bit reading like 8532** from an HS3001 sensor, needs a structured, sequential **language**.

| Layer | Analogy | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Interface** | The meeting room | PMOD, Grove |
| **Bus** | The table and chairs | The wires |
| **Protocol** | The shared language and etiquette | SPI, I2C |

- **Definition:** a protocol is a system of rules that lets two or more devices **transmit and receive information**. It turns a chaotic stream of signals into structured, reliable data.

### 0.1.1 The Three Pillars
- **Syntax (the grammar):** the structure and format, such as message size, start and stop bits, where the address field sits, and payload size.
- **Semantics (the vocabulary):** what each value or field **means**.
- **Timing (the rhythm):** **when** to send and **how fast**, such as the baud rate, clock signals and turn-around times.

### 0.1.2 Why Protocols Matter
- **Interoperability:** a Renesas MCU, a Bosch sensor and a TI ADC can all talk, and no one gets locked into one vendor.
- **Reliability:** checksums, **CRC** and **ACKs** catch corrupted data, which is vital in cars, medical and industrial systems.
- **Efficiency:** a hardware peripheral, such as the RA's I2C or SPI block, handles the protocol and **frees the CPU**.
- **Scalability:** shared buses such as I2C and CAN let you add devices without a redesign.

> [!warning] Both sides must agree
> If an MCU uses **SPI mode 0** and the sensor expects **mode 1**, the sensor samples on the wrong clock edge and the MCU gets **garbage**. A browser's `ERR_SSL_PROTOCOL_ERROR` is the same failure at a higher level: client and server can't agree on a TLS version. **Agreeing on the protocol is a prerequisite**, not an option.

| | Serial | Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| **Data per clock** | 1 bit | Many bits (8, 16, 32 and more) |
| **Wires** | Few, 1 to 4 | Many |
| **Cost** | Low: simple cables, few pins | High: wide cables, many pins |
| **Distance** | Long | Very short, on-board |
| **Noise immunity** | High, especially with **differential** signaling | Low, prone to crosstalk |
| **Clock skew** | **Not an issue** | **The main speed limit** |
| **Complexity** | The protocol must serialize and deserialize | Simple latches, but hard to make reliable at speed |
| **Embedded use** | UART, I2C, SPI, CAN, USB, Ethernet | DDR memory buses, parallel LCD and camera ports, fast ADCs and DACs |

- At the same clock, an 8-bit parallel bus is **in theory 8× faster**. But **clock skew** grows worse with speed: tiny differences in wire length and electrical properties make bits sent together arrive at different times. Once skew is a big part of the clock period, the receiver latches wrong data.
- **The high-speed paradox:** serial has **no skew**, so its clock can be pushed far higher, and it ends up with **more throughput**. That's why USB, SATA and PCIe all replaced parallel buses with serial links.
- Parallel survives where the layout is short and tightly controlled, such as the CPU-to-DDR RAM bus. The legacy examples are the IEEE 1284 printer port and IDE/PATA drives.

> [!note] Correction
> The slides list **PCIe** and **MIPI** camera and display links as parallel interfaces. **PCIe is serial**: each lane is a serial differential pair, and more lanes just add bandwidth. **MIPI CSI-2 and DSI** are serial too. Parallel camera and RGB LCD ports do exist, but they're separate interfaces.

### 0.2.2 Wired vs Wireless
| | Wired | Wireless |
|---|---|---|
| **Speed** | Higher and consistent | Varies with signal strength and interference |
| **Reliability** | Very high, immune to RF interference | Lower: interference, walls, distance |
| **Security** | High, needs physical access | Lower: signals are broadcast, so they need strong encryption (WPA3, AES) |
| **Mobility** | Tethered | **The main advantage** |
| **Installation** | Cabling is costly | Simpler and often cheaper |
| **Latency** | Low and predictable | Higher and variable |

- Wired versus wireless is **independent of serial versus parallel**, but **a wireless link is inherently serial**.
- **Wired** suits fixed systems that need speed, reliability and security, such as industrial control. **Wireless** suits devices that move or are hard to cable, such as IoT nodes.
- The protocols ahead fall into four groups:
  - on-board: **UART, SPI, I2C**
  - robust wired networking: **CAN**
  - system and PC links: **USB, Ethernet**
  - wireless IoT: **BLE, Wi-Fi, NFC, Zigbee**.

## 0.3 On-Board Serial Protocols
- **Synchronous** protocols (SPI, I2C) share a **clock line** that keeps sender and receiver in step. **Asynchronous** ones (UART) have **no clock**, so both sides agree on the timing in advance.

### 0.3.1 UART: The Asynchronous Workhorse
- **History:**
  - 1960s: linking computers such as the DEC PDP to **teletypewriters**
  - about 1971: Western Digital's **WD1402A**, one of the first single-chip UARTs
  - early 1980s: the National Semiconductor **8250** in the first IBM PC
  - 1990s: the **16550** added **FIFO buffers**, so the CPU could hand over data in chunks without losing bytes.
- PCs replaced serial ports with USB, but UART is in **virtually every MCU**. Renesas calls it the **SCI**. A **USB-to-UART bridge** connects it to a PC.
- **Wiring:** **TX of one device goes to RX of the other**, plus a shared ground. Two signal wires give **full duplex**, sending and receiving at the same time.

- **The frame** is how UART creates structure without a clock:
  - the line **idles HIGH**
  - a **start bit (LOW)** marks the beginning
  - **5 to 9 data bits** follow, **LSB first**
  - an optional **parity bit** gives simple error checking (even or odd)
  - **1 or 2 stop bits (HIGH)** end the frame.
- **Baud rate** is the bit rate in bits per second, so it sets each bit's duration. Common values are **9600** for simple devices, 19200, 57600, and **115200** for debugging and faster transfers.

> [!abstract] The golden rule of UART
> Both ends must use the **same baud rate and the same frame format**. In practice the two clocks must match within a few percent, or later bits get sampled in the wrong place and the data turns to garbage.

- **Throughput:** at 115200 baud with **8N1** (8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit), each byte takes 10 bits, so the link carries **11,520 bytes per second**.

### 0.3.2 SPI: The Synchronous Speedster
- **History:** Motorola created SPI in the **mid-1980s** for its MCUs.
  - It was **never formally standardized**, so it's a **de facto** standard. That's why pin names and "flavors" vary, such as SDO/SDI and COPI/CIPO.
  - **Dual and Quad SPI** add 2 or 4 data lines for throughput, popular for flash memory.
  - Renesas **RSPI** in RA and RX MCUs adds bigger buffers and a **command sequencer** that offloads the CPU.
- **Synchronous:** the **master drives the clock (SCK)**, so no baud rate has to be agreed, and **much higher speeds** are possible.
- **Full duplex:** on every clock pulse, one bit goes each way at the same time.
- **The four lines:**
  - **SCK**, the clock, driven by the master
  - **MOSI** (master out, slave in)
  - **MISO** (master in, slave out)
  - **SS/CS** (slave or chip select), which is **active LOW**.
- **Several slaves** share SCK, MOSI and MISO, but each needs its **own CS line**. So the pin count is 3 shared lines plus one CS per device.
- **A transaction:**
  1. Pull the slave's CS **LOW**.
  2. Clock out the bits: each pulse sends a bit on MOSI and receives one on MISO.
  3. Pull CS **HIGH** to end it.

| SPI mode | CPOL | CPHA | Clock idles | Data sampled on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | 0 | 0 | LOW | Leading (first) edge |
| **1** | 0 | 1 | LOW | Trailing (second) edge |
| **2** | 1 | 0 | HIGH | Leading edge |
| **3** | 1 | 1 | HIGH | Trailing edge |

- **CPOL** (clock polarity) sets the clock's idle level, and **CPHA** (clock phase) sets which edge samples the data. Master and slave **must use the same mode**.

### 0.3.3 I2C: The Efficient Two-Wire Bus
- **History:**
  - Philips (now NXP) invented I2C in the early 1980s to **cut the wiring inside TV sets**.
  - The **1982** original ran at **100 kbit/s (Standard Mode)** with **7-bit addresses**, about **112 usable devices**.
  - The **1992** public spec added **Fast Mode, 400 kbit/s**, and 10-bit addressing.
  - Later came Fast Mode Plus (**1 Mbit/s**), High-speed Mode (**3.4 Mbit/s**) and Ultra-fast Mode (**5 Mbit/s**).
- It's a **synchronous, multi-master, multi-slave** bus on just **two shared wires**: **SCL** (clock) and **SDA** (data).
- It's **open-drain**: devices can only pull a line LOW, and **pull-up resistors** hold it HIGH when idle. That prevents bus fights and makes multiple masters possible.
- Instead of chip-select lines, I2C uses **software addresses**.

- **A transaction:**
  1. **Start:** the master pulls SDA LOW while SCL is HIGH.
  2. **Address:** a 7-bit slave address plus a **read/write bit** (0 = write, 1 = read).
  3. **ACK/NACK:** the addressed slave pulls SDA LOW for one clock to acknowledge. If nobody does, it's a NACK.
  4. **Data:** 8-bit bytes, each followed by an ACK.
  5. **Stop:** the master releases SDA HIGH while SCL is HIGH.
- **Multi-master arbitration:** if two masters start together, the open-drain bus acts as a **wired-AND**. A master that sends a 1 (releases the line) but sees a 0 knows it **lost**, stops at once and waits. The winner carries on with **no data corrupted**.

### 0.3.4 UART vs SPI vs I2C
| Feature | UART | SPI | I2C |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wires** | 2 + GND | 3 shared + 1 CS per slave, + GND | 2 + GND |
| **Clock** | None (asynchronous) | Master-driven | Shared SCL |
| **Speed** | Slowest, up to about 1 Mbps | **Fastest**, 10+ Mbps | 100 kbps, 400 kbps, 1 Mbps and up |
| **Addressing** | None, point-to-point | A hardware CS line per slave | **Software 7 or 10-bit address** |
| **Duplex** | Full | Full | Half |
| **Complexity** | Simple | Moderate | Higher: addressing, ACK/NACK |
| **Renesas peripheral** | SCI | RSPI, SCI | RIIC, SCI |

### 0.3.5 Example: A Wireless Environmental Monitor
An **RA6E2 QuickConnect board** uses all three protocols at once:
1. It reads temperature and humidity from an **HS3001 Pmod over I2C**.
2. It reads motion from an **SPI accelerometer Pmod**.
3. It packs both readings into one packet.
4. It sends the packet **over UART** to a **DA16600 Pmod**, which forwards it wirelessly to a phone or PC.

> [!note] Correction
> The course calls the **DA16600** a BLE module. It's a **Wi-Fi and Bluetooth LE combo module**: Wi-Fi for data, with BLE often used for setup.

## 0.4 CAN: Robust Wired Networking
**Why the on-board protocols fail in cars and factories:**
- **Noise:** single-ended signals such as UART are vulnerable to the strong **EMI** from motors and ignition systems.
- **Distance:** they aren't built to run meters across a vehicle or factory floor.
- **Scalability:** SPI needs a chip-select per device, which becomes a **wiring nightmare** with dozens of nodes.
- **No priority:** a radio-volume message mustn't delay an **airbag** message. A modern car has **over 70 ECUs**.

| Year | Milestone |
|---|---|
| **1983 to 1986** | **Bosch** develops CAN to end the wiring nightmare in cars, and unveils it at the **SAE Congress in 1986** |
| **1991** | **CAN 2.0** defines **11-bit (2.0A)** and **29-bit (2.0B)** identifiers |
| **1993** | It becomes the international standard **ISO 11898** |
| **2012** | **CAN FD** (flexible data rate) raises the payload from **8 to 64 bytes** and speeds up the data phase to about **5 Mbps**, for ADAS and infotainment |
| **Next** | **CAN XL**: payloads up to **2048 bytes** at about **20 Mbps**, bridging the gap to automotive Ethernet |

- CAN uses a **twisted pair**, **CAN_H and CAN_L**, with **differential signaling**. The bit value comes from the **voltage difference** between the wires, so noise that hits both equally (**common-mode noise**) cancels out.
- A **dominant 0** actively drives the bus and **overrides a recessive 1**, which is passive. This is the key to arbitration.
- A **120 Ω terminating resistor at each end** of the bus stops reflections.

### 0.4.2 Message-Based, Not Address-Based
- There's **no master**. Every message is **broadcast** to all nodes.
- Each message's **identifier (ID)** describes its **content**, such as "engine RPM", **not its destination**. Each node's hardware **acceptance filters** keep only the messages it cares about.

- **Priority-based arbitration:** nodes that start together send their IDs bit by bit and **listen as they send**.
  - A node that sends a recessive 1 but sees a dominant 0 knows a **higher-priority (lower ID)** message is on the bus. It stops at once and becomes a listener.
  - The winning message carries on uncorrupted. This is **non-destructive arbitration**, and it guarantees that **urgent messages always get through first**.

### 0.4.3 Error Handling
CAN is famous for reliability. Every controller checks for errors **five ways**:
1. **Bit monitoring:** a transmitter checks that the bus shows the bit it sent.
2. **Bit stuffing:** after 5 identical bits, a bit of the opposite value is inserted. **Six identical bits** mean a **stuff error**. Stuffing also gives receivers regular edges to stay in sync.
3. **Frame check:** the fixed-format parts of the frame must be correct.
4. **ACK check:** at least one receiver must acknowledge the frame.
5. **CRC:** a **15-bit checksum** protects the content.

- **Error signaling:** any node that spots an error sends an **error frame**, which breaks the stuffing rule on purpose and **destroys the message**, so no node acts on bad data.
- **Fault confinement:** each node counts its transmit and receive errors (TEC and REC).
  - **Error active** is the normal state.
  - It becomes **error passive** when a count passes **127**. Then it can no longer destroy traffic.
  - It goes **bus off** when **TEC passes 255**. A clearly faulty node takes itself offline to protect the network.

### 0.4.4 Example: A Bottling Plant
- **Nodes:** a PLC, motor controllers, optical sensors, and the filling, capping and labeling stations.
- **Why CAN:**
  - **heavy EMI** from big motors, which differential signaling handles
  - **precisely timed actions**, which CAN's determinism makes possible
  - **dozens of distributed nodes** along the line.
- **In action:** an optical sensor broadcasts **ID 0x201, "Bottle_Present"**.
  - The **PLC** updates its tracking log.
  - The **filling station** gets ready to fill.
  - The **capping station** ignores the message.
- The Renesas **RX660** suits this: **5 V operation** for noise tolerance and built-in **CAN FD**.

## 0.5 USB and Ethernet
### 0.5.1 USB
- USB **standardizes how peripherals connect**, and carries both data and power.
- It's **asymmetric**: the **host** runs the bus, supplies power and **starts all communication**, and **devices** only respond.
- It uses a **tiered-star** topology of up to **127 devices**.

| Version | Year | Top speed |
|---|---|---|
| **USB 1.x** | 1996 | Low Speed 1.5 Mbit/s, Full Speed 12 Mbit/s |
| **USB 2.0** | 2000 | High Speed **480 Mbit/s** |
| **USB 3.x** | 2008 on | SuperSpeed **5 to 20 Gbit/s** |
| **USB4** | 2019 on | 40 Gbit/s, and **80 Gbit/s** with USB4 version 2.0 (2022) |

- **Enumeration:** when a device is plugged in, it gives the host its **descriptors**, which list its capabilities and power needs. The host then configures it.
- **USB On-The-Go (OTG)** lets a device such as a phone **switch roles** and act as a host.
- **Pipes** are logical channels from the host to **endpoints**, the data sources and sinks inside a device.

| Transfer type | Use | Character | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Control** | Configuring the device | Bidirectional, always on **endpoint 0** | Enumeration |
| **Interrupt** | Small, periodic data | Low latency, polled by the host | Keyboard, mouse |
| **Isochronous** | Real-time streams | **Guaranteed timing**, no retries | Audio |
| **Bulk** | Large transfers | Error-checked, uses spare bandwidth | Printer, scanner, storage |

- **Strengths:**
  - **Power delivery** up to **240 W** with USB-PD 3.1
  - **hot-swapping** and **self-configuration**
  - the universal **Type-C** connector
  - **backward compatibility**.
- **Limits:**
  - cables up to **5 m** for USB 2.0 High Speed
  - **host-driven**: devices can't talk directly to each other without a host.

### 0.5.2 Ethernet
- Ethernet is the backbone of wired networks, standardized as **IEEE 802.3**. It covers OSI **layer 1 (physical)** and **layer 2 (data link)**.
- It's **best effort**, with no guaranteed delivery. Higher layers such as TCP add reliability. Early shared Ethernet had to handle **collisions**, but modern switched, full-duplex links don't have them.
- **Topologies:**
  - **star**: the most common, around a central switch, easy to troubleshoot
  - **ring**: each device links to two others, which gives **redundancy**
  - **line (bus)**: one cable, one point of failure
  - **mesh**: many links each, very reliable but costly
  - **hybrid**: a mix of the others.

**Industrial Ethernet** bridges office IT and plant **OT (operational technology)**. It adds:
- **determinism**, meaning guaranteed timely delivery for control loops
- **ruggedized hardware** that survives heat, humidity, vibration and EMI
- real-time protocols, **quality of service (QoS)** and prioritization
- **redundant** ring, bus and star topologies
- **remote diagnostics** and central management.

| Protocol | Key point |
|---|---|
| **PROFINET** | A real-time channel (RT and IRT), popular in Europe |
| **EtherNet/IP** | Built on CIP over standard Ethernet, popular in the USA |
| **Modbus TCP/IP** | Older, wraps Modbus in TCP/IP, widely used for SCADA |
| **EtherCAT** | Real-time "telegram" frames processed on the fly as they pass each node |

### 0.6.1 Bluetooth and BLE
- Bluetooth was born in **1998**. It works in the **2.4 GHz ISM band** and dodges interference by **frequency hopping** about **1600 times a second**.
- The **Bluetooth SIG**, founded in 1998, develops and licenses the standard and has over 40,000 member companies.

| | Bluetooth Classic | Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) |
|---|---|---|
| **Since** | 1998 | 2010 (Bluetooth 4.0) |
| **Data rate** | Up to **3 Mbps** with EDR | **1 to 2 Mbps** |
| **Power** | Higher, for continuous streams | **Ultra-low**: sleeps, wakes for short bursts |
| **Uses** | Headphones, speakers, car kits, file sharing | Fitness trackers, sensors, beacons, remotes, asset tags |

- **How BLE sips power:** a lightweight link layer plus **deep sleep**, waking only for quick data bursts.
  - That lets a **coin cell** power a device **for years**.
  - Peak current is about **5.5 to 15 mA**, against **200 mA or more** for Wi-Fi transmitting.
- **Bluetooth 5** lets you choose a trade-off:
  - **2× speed** with the 2 Mbps PHY
  - **4× range** with the **coded PHY**, which adds error correction. It can pass **1 km** with clear line of sight.

> [!note] Correction
> The slides claim Bluetooth 5.0 gives Classic "boost speeds up to 50 Mbps". It doesn't. Classic tops out at **3 Mbps** with EDR, and Bluetooth 5's fast mode is **BLE at 2 Mbps**. The old Bluetooth 3.0 + HS reached 24 Mbps only by handing data to a Wi-Fi radio.

### 0.6.2 Wi-Fi
- Wi-Fi is standardized as **IEEE 802.11** and is the **high-throughput** option. It uses three bands:
  - **2.4 GHz**: longer range, better through walls
  - **5 GHz**: more bandwidth, less interference
  - **6 GHz**: the most spectrum.

| Generation | Standard | Year | Peak rate | Key technology |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Original | 802.11 | 1997 | 2 Mbps | |
| **Wi-Fi 4** | 802.11n | 2009 | 600 Mbps | **MIMO**: several antennas |
| **Wi-Fi 5** | 802.11ac | 2014 | Over 1 Gbps | MU-MIMO, mainly 5 GHz |
| **Wi-Fi 6** | 802.11ax | 2019 | 9.6 Gbps | **OFDMA**: up to 4× average throughput per user in crowded places |
| **Wi-Fi 7** | 802.11be | 2024 | 46 Gbps | **Multi-Link Operation (MLO)**, **320 MHz** channels |

- **OFDMA** splits one channel into many small slices so several devices transmit at once, like a teacher giving each student their own pitch in a noisy classroom.
- **MLO** connects over **2.4, 5 and 6 GHz at the same time**, for speed and fail-safe reliability.
- Wi-Fi fits **high-bandwidth** devices such as HD security cameras and smart TVs, and **dense** homes and offices. Over **3.2 billion** Wi-Fi devices shipped in 2021.
- The **Wi-Fi Alliance**, formed in 1999, owns the trademark and **certifies that devices work together**.

> [!note] Correction
> The slides tie the **6 GHz band to Wi-Fi 7**. It arrived earlier, with **Wi-Fi 6E**.

### 0.6.3 NFC
- **Near-Field Communication** works at **13.56 MHz** through **inductive coupling** between two loop antennas.
- Its range is **4 cm or less**, which is a **security feature**, not a limitation.
- Its data rate is low, **106 to 848 kbit/s**, but setup is **near-instant, about 0.1 s**.
- **Its three modes:**
  - **card emulation:** the phone acts as a smart card, which is how **mobile payments** work
  - **reader/writer:** reads or writes cheap, **unpowered tags** in posters, products and access cards
  - **peer-to-peer:** direct exchange between two active devices.
- **Bootstrapping:** a tap exchanges keys and addresses to set up a **faster Bluetooth or Wi-Fi Direct link**.
- **NFC wireless charging (WLC)** delivers up to **1 W** over about 2 cm, enough for earbuds and styluses.
- The **NFC Forum** keeps it interoperable.

### 0.6.4 Zigbee
- Zigbee is built on **IEEE 802.15.4** in the 2.4 GHz band, and forms a **self-healing mesh** that extends range by **relaying through nodes**.
- **Its roles:**
  - **coordinator:** one per network. It forms the network, manages the security keys and is the root.
  - **router:** relays traffic and is usually mains-powered.
  - **end device:** a simple sensor that talks only to its parent and **deep-sleeps**, for battery life of 2 years or more.
- It scales to **about 65,000 nodes** and uses **AES-128** encryption. Zigbee 3.0 adds install codes.
- It's used in smart homes (Philips Hue, SmartThings), sensor networks and industrial control. The **Connectivity Standards Alliance (CSA)** standardizes it.

### 0.6.5 The Right Tool for the Job
| Protocol | Pick it for |
|---|---|
| **Wi-Fi** | High-speed data, dense environments |
| **Zigbee** | Self-healing mesh, massive scale |
| **Bluetooth / BLE** | Ultra-low power, personal-area networks |
| **NFC** | Instant, secure tap-and-go |
