> [!abstract] Key idea
> **Peripherals** are the MCU's senses and limbs: dedicated hardware that links the CPU to the outside world **without tying the CPU up**. This module covers how they evolved, then the core ones in depth: **ADCs and DACs, timers and GPIO**.

## 0.1 Peripherals: The MCU's Senses and Limbs
- An MCU has a **CPU** (the brain), **memory** (RAM for short-term data, flash/ROM for the program) and a **bus system** (the nervous system that links them).
- A brain in a jar is useless. **Peripherals** are the specialized hardware that lets the CPU **sense its environment and act on it**.
- **Why not do everything with GPIO?** Driving a protocol by toggling pins in software (**bit-banging**) is slow, inefficient and keeps the CPU busy. Hardware interfaces **handle the protocol automatically**.
  - **UART:** asynchronous serial, like a simple two-wire telegraph, for PCs and GPS modules.
  - **I2C:** a two-wire bus linking one MCU to many sensors.
  - **SPI:** a fast synchronous bus for high-bandwidth devices such as displays and flash memory.

### 0.1.1 How the MCU Was Born
- **Early 1970s:** the first microprocessors, such as the Intel 4004, were **just CPUs**. A system needed many external chips and cost hundreds of dollars, which was too much for small appliances.
- **The catalyst:** the consumer calculator market demanded more integration to cut cost and power.

| Year | Milestone | What it integrated |
|---|---|---|
| **1974** | **TI TMS1000**, the first commercial MCU | CPU, ROM, RAM and I/O on one chip, about **2 US dollars in volume**. Its I/O was application-specific: "K" lines for scanning keyboards, "R" and "O" lines for driving displays |
| **1977** | **Intel 8048** | RAM, ROM/EPROM, **general-purpose I/O ports** and an 8-bit timer/counter |
| **1980s on** | 8-bit MCUs: 8051, PIC, today's RL78 | Standard peripherals: UART, basic ADCs, more timers |
| **Today** | 32-bit MCUs: Arm Cortex-M (Renesas RA) and Renesas RX | Complex, autonomous peripherals: fast ADCs and DACs, DMA, USB, Ethernet, CAN |

### 0.1.2 Packages and Their Trade-offs
| Era | Package | Mounting | Pin density | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1960s to 70s | **DIP** (dual in-line) | Through-hole | Low, 40 to 64 pins at most | Prototyping, hobby use |
| 1980s | **QFP** (quad flat) | Surface mount, leads on four sides | Medium | General-purpose SMT boards |
| 1990s | **BGA** (ball grid array) | Solder balls underneath | Very high, hundreds of pins | High performance, high I/O |
| Today | **WLCSP** (wafer-level chip-scale) | The package is the size of the die | Highest for its size | Extreme miniaturization |

- **Pin count vs board space:** more peripherals need more pins. Dense packages save space but add complexity.
- **Heat:** powerful MCUs get hot. A **QFN's exposed thermal pad** or a BGA's many balls spread heat far better.
- **Cost vs complexity:** chip cost = **die + package + test**. A simple package works on a cheap 2-layer PCB, but a BGA needs an **expensive multi-layer PCB** and precise assembly.

### 0.1.3 Selecting an MCU in Industry
Choosing an MCU starts from the application and works down. This repeats the checklist in Module 2.
1. **Define the system requirements:** the end product and its tasks.
2. **Processing needs:** is an 8-bit MCU enough, or do the algorithms need 32-bit performance?
3. **Peripherals:** count the UARTs, I2C/SPI ports, ADC channels and timers needed. This sets the **minimum pin count**.
4. **Power budget:** a battery IoT device needs ultra-low power, while a wall-powered controller can use a high-performance part.
5. **Ecosystem:** tools, libraries and community support **drastically cut development time**.
6. **Total system cost and supply chain:** the cheapest MCU isn't always best. Count the PCB, assembly and **long-term availability** too.

## 0.2 ADCs and DACs
### 0.2.1 Analog vs Digital
| | Analog signal | Digital signal |
|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | Continuous in time and amplitude | Discrete in time (**sampled**) and amplitude (**quantized**) |
| **Represents** | The physical quantity directly: voltage, pressure, sound | A sequence of discrete values, usually binary |
| **Strength** | Unlimited resolution within its range | **Robust against noise**, easy to compute, store and error-correct |
| **Weakness** | Picks up noise and degrades in transmission | Limited resolution |

- The physical world is analog, but computation is digital. **Data converters are the bridge:** sensor → **ADC** → processor → **DAC** → actuator.

### 0.2.2 A Short History
- The **telegraph and telephone** industries needed to fit many channels onto one wire, which drove research into sampling and encoding.
- **1921:** Paul Rainey patented the first electronic ideas for a flash ADC and a resistive DAC.
- **1937:** **Alec Reeves** invented **pulse code modulation (PCM)**, with the first all-electronic ADC and DAC built from vacuum tubes.
- **1920s to 1940s:** **Nyquist and Shannon** gave sampling theory its mathematical foundation.
- **1947 and 1958:** the transistor and the integrated circuit made the technology practical.
- **1954:** the **Datrac**, the first commercial ADC: 11 bits, 50 kSPS, successive approximation, vacuum tubes, **500 W and a full equipment rack**.
- **1969 to 1978:** converters moved onto single chips:
  - 1969: the PMI **DAC01**, the first monolithic 6-bit DAC
  - 1974: the **AD7520**, the first monolithic 10-bit CMOS DAC
  - 1976: the **AD561**, the first laser-trimmed 10-bit DAC with an on-chip reference
  - 1978: the **AD571**, the first complete single-chip 10-bit ADC.

### 0.2.3 Performance Metrics
**Static metrics** describe accuracy with DC or slowly changing inputs:
- **Resolution (N bits)** gives **2^N levels**, and the step size is **1 LSB = Vref / 2^N**.
- **Offset error** is a constant shift of the whole transfer curve. **Gain error** is a slope error that grows with the input.
- **DNL (differential non-linearity)** is how far each code step's width differs from the ideal 1 LSB. If **DNL ≤ −1 LSB**, the ADC has a **missing code** it will never output.
- **INL (integral non-linearity)** is the largest deviation of the whole curve from a straight line, after removing offset and gain errors. It's the **main linearity measure** and sets the distortion.
- **TUE (total unadjusted error)** combines offset, gain and linearity errors into one number.

**Dynamic metrics** describe AC inputs, measured with an FFT:
- **Quantization noise** is the error from rounding to discrete levels. Its RMS value is **q/√12**, where q is 1 LSB.
- **SNR** is signal power over noise power. For an ideal N-bit converter, **SNR = 6.02 × N + 1.76 dB**, so 12 bits gives about **74 dB** and 16 bits about **98 dB**.
- **THD (total harmonic distortion)** is the power in the harmonics, caused by non-linearity.
- **SINAD** combines noise and distortion.
- **ENOB (effective number of bits) = (SINAD − 1.76) / 6.02.** For example, a 12-bit ADC with a SINAD of 68 dB behaves like an **11-bit** one.

### 0.2.4 Sampling: Nyquist and Aliasing
- **Nyquist-Shannon:** to rebuild a signal, sample at **at least twice its highest frequency**, so fs ≥ 2 × fmax. The frequency **fs/2** is the **Nyquist frequency**.
- **Aliasing:** an input above fs/2 isn't lost. It **folds back** and appears as a fake frequency, **f_alias = |k × fs − fin|**. Sample a 900 Hz tone at 1 kHz and you see a **100 Hz** tone that's impossible to tell from a real one.
- **The anti-aliasing filter (AAF)** is an analog **low-pass filter in front of the ADC**:
  - its **passband** is flat from 0 to fmax
  - it **attenuates** everything above fs/2 below the ADC's noise floor
  - **oversampling** widens the gap between fmax and fs/2, so a simpler, cheaper filter will do.

For a given technology and power budget, **sample rate and resolution trade off against each other**. No single architecture is best.

| Architecture | Speed | Resolution | Power/cost | Principle | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Flash** | Fastest (GSPS) | Low (≤ 12 bits) | Very high | Parallel comparators | Oscilloscopes, software-defined radio |
| **Pipelined** | Very fast (MSPS to GSPS) | Medium (12 to 16) | High | Staged conversion | High-speed data acquisition, comms |
| **SAR** | Medium (kSPS to MSPS) | Medium-high (12 to 20) | Low | Binary search | Industrial control, BMS |
| **Sigma-delta (ΣΔ)** | Slowest (SPS to kSPS) | Highest (16 to 32) | Very low | Oversampling and noise shaping | Pro audio, precision measurement |
| **Dual-slope** | Very slow (SPS) | High (≤ 22) | Low | Analog integration | Digital voltmeters |

#### 0.2.5.1 SAR: The Workhorse
- Its four blocks:
  - a **sample-and-hold** that freezes the input
  - a **comparator**
  - an **internal N-bit DAC**
  - the **successive approximation register (SAR)** logic.
- It runs a **binary search**, one bit per clock from MSB to LSB, so an N-bit result takes **N cycles**. Each cycle it sets the next bit to 1, the DAC outputs that guess, and the comparator decides whether to keep or clear the bit.
- It gives **8 to 20 bits at up to about 10 MSPS** with very low power.
- It has **no pipeline delay**, so one ADC can be **multiplexed** across many channels and used in control loops.

#### 0.2.5.2 Sigma-Delta: The High-Resolution Specialist
- **Oversampling:** a fast **1-bit modulator** samples many times faster than the output rate. The quantization noise stays the same in total but spreads over a much wider band, so less of it lands in the signal band.
- **Noise shaping:** an **integrator in a feedback loop** pushes the noise up to high frequencies, away from the signal.
- **Digital filter and decimation:** a steep low-pass filter, often a **sinc** filter, removes that noise. Then the stream is **downsampled** to the output rate, **trading speed for resolution**.
- **Payoff:**
  - Each doubling of the oversampling ratio gives **+3 dB (0.5 bit)** from oversampling alone.
  - With a first-order modulator it gives **+9 dB (1.5 bits)**.
  - Higher-order modulators give even more.
- The output is a 1-bit stream whose **density of 1s** follows the input.
- It's **inherently monotonic**, and oversampling gives **built-in anti-aliasing**.

#### 0.2.5.3 Flash: The Speed Demon
- A resistor string makes **2^N − 1 reference levels**, and the same number of comparators check the input against them **all at once**, in one clock cycle.
- The comparator outputs form a **thermometer code**, such as 00011111. A **priority encoder** turns it into binary.
- It reaches **GSPS** with zero latency. But the comparator count **doubles with every bit**: 255 for 8 bits and 4095 for 12. So power, die area and input capacitance explode, and flash ADCs stop at about **8 to 12 bits**.

### 0.2.6 DAC Architectures
- A DAC does the inverse: an ideal DAC outputs **Vout = Vref × D / 2^N**, a perfect staircase of 1 LSB steps.
- The same DNL, INL, offset and gain errors apply. **Monotonicity**, meaning the output never goes down when the code goes up, is critical.
- **Binary-weighted resistor DAC:** resistors of R, 2R, 4R, 8R and so on feed an inverting summing op-amp, one switch per bit.
  - It's simple, but a 12-bit version needs a **2048:1 resistor ratio**, such as 1 kΩ to 2.048 MΩ.
  - That range is nearly impossible to make precisely on a chip.
- **R-2R ladder DAC:** uses **only two resistor values**, R and 2R.
  - From any node, the ladder looks like **R**, so each bit's current halves naturally.
  - With the feedback resistor equal to R, the MSB contributes −Vref/2 and the output is **Vout = −Vref × D / 2^N**.
  - Matched identical resistors are easy to make, so the R-2R ladder is the **dominant precision DAC**. The DACs in MCUs like the **RA4M1** use it.
- **String DAC:** a string of identical resistors with a switch tap for each code. It's **inherently monotonic** and simple, and is used in digital potentiometers.

### 0.2.7 Matching the Converter to the Application
| Application | Needs | Best architecture |
|---|---|---|
| **High-fidelity audio** | Dynamic range above 96 dB (more than 16 bits), THD below 0.001%, 20 Hz to 20 kHz, sampling at 44.1, 96 or 192 kHz | **Sigma-delta**, often 24-bit |
| **Industrial control (PLC)** | Dozens of sensors, low latency for closed loops, 12 to 18 bits at 100 kSPS to 5 MSPS, low power | **SAR** behind an analog multiplexer |
| **High-speed instruments (oscilloscopes)** | Signals of hundreds of MHz or GHz, GSPS sampling, 8 to 12 bits | **Flash** |

- In a **PLC analog input module**, the MCU steps a multiplexer through the sensors and triggers one **zero-latency SAR ADC** for each. That's why RA MCUs with fast SAR ADCs are common in PLCs and motor controllers.
- **Audio chain:** microphone → preamp → ΣΔ ADC → DSP, then on playback ΣΔ DAC → analog filter → power amp → speaker.

## 0.3 Timers
### 0.3.1 Why Hardware Timers
- A **software delay loop** blocks the CPU, like a chef staring at the clock while the cake bakes.
- A **hardware timer** is an independent circuit that runs **in parallel with the CPU**. The CPU sets and starts it, does other work, and gets an **interrupt when time is up**, like a chef with a kitchen timer.
- Timekeeping evolved from **hourglasses and cam timers**, where shape encoded the logic, to **quartz-based programmable timers**, and finally to timer peripherals built into every MCU.

- **Clock source:** the tick, from the system clock or an external pin.
- **Prescaler:** a configurable frequency divider. A prescaler of 64 means one count per 64 clock ticks, so the timer can **measure longer intervals**.
- **Counter register (TCNT):** an 8, 16 or 32-bit counter. A 16-bit counter goes from 0 to 65,535, then **overflows** back to 0, which usually fires the interrupt. Clocked straight from 60 MHz, it would overflow every **1.09 ms**, so a prescaler stretches that to seconds.
- **Control and status registers:** the software interface to pick the mode, start and stop the timer, and read its flags.

| | Polling | Interrupts |
|---|---|---|
| **How** | The CPU keeps checking a status flag | The hardware calls an **ISR** when the event happens |
| **CPU use** | High, busy-waiting | Very low, free until the interrupt |
| **Responsiveness** | Poor, the CPU is blocked | Excellent |
| **Analogy** | "Are we there yet?" on a road trip | "I'll wake you when we get there" |

### 0.3.3 GPT: The Workhorse
A **General Purpose Timer (GPT)** is a flexible 16 or 32-bit timer **clocked from the fast peripheral clock**, so it has high resolution but also high power. Higher-end parts such as the **RA6** series have more, and 32-bit, GPT channels than the low-power RA2 and RA4 series.

Its modes:
- **Periodic:** an interrupt at a fixed interval, for example to sample a sensor every 10 ms.
- **One-shot:** a single interrupt after a delay, then it stops. It's used for timeouts.
- **Output compare:** when the counter matches a compare value, it fires an interrupt or sets, clears or toggles a pin.
- **Input capture:** on an edge of an external signal, it saves the counter value. It's used to measure pulse widths and frequency.
- **PWM:** output compare that produces a continuous square wave with a programmable **duty cycle**.

> [!abstract] The timer formula
> **T_timer = T_clk × Prescaler × (Autoreload + 1)**, so **Autoreload = T_timer / (T_clk × Prescaler) − 1**. The +1 and −1 are there because the count includes zero.

- **Example 1, a 100 ms interrupt** on an RA6M3 with a 60 MHz peripheral clock (PCLKD) and a 32-bit GPT:
  - T_clk = 1/60 MHz = **16.67 ns**
  - with a prescaler of 64, one tick is **1.067 µs**
  - 100 ms / 1.067 µs = **93,750 ticks**, so the autoreload value is **93,749**, well within 32 bits.
- **Example 2, a 25 kHz PWM at 75% duty** to drive a motor, with the same clock:
  - the period is 1/25 kHz = **40 µs**
  - with a prescaler of 1, for the best duty resolution, that's 40 µs / 16.67 ns = **2400 ticks**, so the autoreload is **2399**
  - the compare value sets the ON time: 0.75 × 2400 = **1800**. The slide's 2399 × 0.75 ≈ 1799 gives 74.96%.

- **PWM** controls the **average power** to a load by varying ON time against OFF time. **V_avg = V_high × duty cycle**, so a 5 V signal at 20% averages **1 V**.
- **GPT applications:**
  - **3-phase BLDC motor control:** three synchronized GPT channels, one per phase (U, V, W), generate phase-shifted PWM. **Dead time** is a short gap between switching off one transistor of a half bridge and switching on the other, which prevents a short circuit (**shoot-through**). The GPT can insert it in hardware, and FSP has an `r_gpt_three_phase` driver.
  - **Servo control:** a 50 Hz PWM whose **1 to 2 ms pulse width** sets the position.
  - **Sound:** output compare toggles a pin to drive a buzzer. A 440 Hz note needs a toggle every half period, 880 times a second.
  - **Frequency and RPM measurement** with input capture.
  - **LED dimming:** the eye sees the average brightness.
  - **Blinking LEDs without blocking:** a periodic 250 ms interrupt whose short ISR just toggles the LED pins, which leaves the main loop free.
- **Renesas FSP** sets up timers in a graphical configurator and generates the initialization code. In periodic mode, the configured **callback** runs automatically on every interrupt.

### 0.3.4 AGT: The Low-Power Timer
- The **Asynchronous General-purpose Timer (AGT)** in Renesas RA MCUs runs from a **separate low-speed clock**: the **32.768 kHz sub-clock crystal** or the **LOCO** (low-speed on-chip oscillator).
- It **keeps counting in deep Software Standby**, when the CPU and most peripherals are off, so it can **wake the system**.

| | GPT (generalist) | AGT (specialist) |
|---|---|---|
| **Pros** | High speed, high resolution, rich features such as advanced PWM | **Extremely low power**, works in deep sleep |
| **Cons** | High power, tied to the main clock | Slower, lower resolution, simpler |
| **Use for** | Speed and precision | Battery life and long wake-up intervals |

- **Example, waking an RA2E1 every 10 s** with a 16-bit AGT clocked by the LOCO:
  - LOCO 32.768 kHz ÷ prescaler 128 = **256 Hz**
  - 10 s × 256 = **2560 ticks**, so the period register gets **2559**, well within 16 bits.

- **Low-power IoT sensor:** a battery node for agriculture or asset tracking must run for **years**. The radio is power-hungry but needed only briefly. The cycle is:
  1. **Sleep:** the MCU sets the AGT for the next interval and enters Software Standby, drawing µA or even nA.
  2. **Wake:** the AGT overflows and its interrupt wakes the MCU.
  3. **Transmit:** the MCU powers the sensor, takes a reading, powers the radio, sends the data, then powers everything down.
  4. **Repeat:** back to step 1.
- **Other AGT uses:**
  - **counting external pulses** while the MCU sleeps, such as a utility meter's sensor, then waking now and then to read the total
  - **gated counting**, where two AGTs make a simple low-power **quadrature encoder** interface
  - generating the **IR remote-control carrier** together with a serial port (SCI).

- A **WDT** is a **safety mechanism, not a general timer**. It's a **dead man's switch** that detects and recovers from software faults such as freezes and infinite loops.
- It always counts down. The application must **"kick" or "feed"** it periodically to reset it. If the software hangs, the WDT times out and forces a **hardware reset** back to a known good state.
- **Internal vs external:**
  - An **internal WDT** costs nothing, but a software bug or power glitch could disable it along with the CPU. This is a **common-cause failure**.
  - An **external WDT** chip costs more but gives **true redundancy**. This fail-safe design is the bedrock of safety standards such as **ISO 26262**.
- **Advanced types:**
  - A **windowed WDT** accepts a kick only **inside a time window**. Too late means a freeze, and **too early** means a task stuck in a fast loop that isn't doing its full job.
  - A **challenge-response WDT** sends a random number, and the MCU must return the correct calculation. This proves the code runs **correctly**, not just that it runs.
- In FSP, the `r_wdt` module sets the timeout, the window, and the action on timeout: a **reset or an NMI** (non-maskable interrupt).

### 0.3.6 The Real-Time Clock (RTC)
- An **RTC** tracks the **time of day and the date** in human units, from seconds to years.
- **Its key feature is power independence:** a **coin cell or supercapacitor** and its own **32.768 kHz crystal** keep it running for years with the main power off.
- **32,768 = 2^15**, so a chain of **15 flip-flops** (a 15-stage binary counter) divides it down to exactly **1 Hz**, one pulse per second.
- RTC registers often use **BCD (binary-coded decimal)**: 4 bits per decimal digit, so 23 is stored as **0010 0011**, not the binary 0001 0111. That makes displaying the time easy.
- **RTC vs GPT:** the RTC answers "**what time is it?**", and the GPT answers "**how long did it take?**".
- **Data logging:** each sensor reading is stored with an RTC **timestamp**. Because the RTC survives power cycles, the record stays complete and **auditable**.

## 0.4 GPIO
### 0.4.1 What GPIO Is
- A **GPIO** is an **uncommitted digital pin** that software can set up as an input, an output, or both.
  - **General purpose:** it has no fixed role until the engineer gives it one.
  - **Digital:** it works with two levels, logic 1 (HIGH) and logic 0 (LOW).
  - **Programmable:** software sets its direction and behavior, such as pull-ups and drive strength.
- It's the most basic bridge between the MCU and the physical world. The CPU controls each pin by reading and writing **hardware registers**.
- **How I/O evolved:**
  1. dedicated **I/O processors** on early computers
  2. fixed-purpose ports, such as the **Centronics** parallel printer port
  3. the **Intel 8255 PPI**: 24 pins in three 8-bit ports, configured by a control word, with basic, strobed and bidirectional modes. Renesas still makes a CMOS version.
  4. **on-chip ports** on MCUs such as the **Intel 8051**, which is modern GPIO.

### 0.4.2 Port-Mapped vs Memory-Mapped I/O
| | Port-mapped I/O (PMIO) | Memory-mapped I/O (MMIO) |
|---|---|---|
| **Address space** | Separate from memory | Peripheral registers sit **in the memory map** |
| **Instructions** | Special ones, such as IN and OUT on x86 | Ordinary loads and stores; a register reads like a variable |
| **Analogy** | Homes have street addresses, mail goes to P.O. box numbers | Every building, post office included, shares one street system |
| **Where** | x86 PCs | **Arm Cortex-M MCUs** such as the RA6E2 |

- **Control logic** connects to the bus and sets every other part from register values.
- The **push-pull output driver** has a P-MOS to VCC that **sources** current to drive HIGH, and an N-MOS to GND that **sinks** current to drive LOW.
- The **Schmitt trigger input** has **hysteresis**, which cleans up noisy or slow edges into a stable logic level.
- **Pull-up and pull-down resistors** are switchable resistors that give an input a **default level** when nothing drives it.
- **ESD diodes** clamp electrostatic discharge spikes to protect the fragile logic.

### 0.4.4 Floating Inputs and Pull Resistors
- An input left unconnected, such as a button whose switch is open, is **floating**. It acts like an **antenna**, its level drifts randomly, and the input flips between HIGH and LOW.
- This causes **spurious interrupts, false readings and extra power**, because a mid-level input turns on both transistors of the input buffer at once.
- **Pull-up:** a resistor to VCC makes the pin read **1** by default. A pressed button to GND overpowers it, and the pin reads **0**. The resistor also **limits the current**, so it isn't a short circuit.
- **Pull-down:** a resistor to GND makes the default **0**, and a button to VCC makes it **1**.
- Modern MCUs like the RA6E2 have **weak internal pull-ups** you enable in software, so you often need no external part.

> [!tip] Sizing a pull-up
> - A **small R (strong pull-up)** gives fast rise times and good noise immunity, but **wastes power when the line is held LOW: P = V²/R**. At 3.3 V, a 10 kΩ pull-up burns **1.1 mW** and a 4.7 kΩ one **2.3 mW**.
> - A **large R (weak pull-up)** saves power but rises slowly, because R forms an RC delay with the line capacitance, and it picks up noise more easily.
> - **Limits:** R must be **small enough** to reach VIH despite leakage current, and **large enough** that a device pulling LOW reaches VIL and stays within its sink current (IOL).
> - **4.7 kΩ to 10 kΩ** suits most slow signals. For **I2C**, calculate it from the bus capacitance and speed.

### 0.4.5 Push-Pull vs Open-Drain
| | Push-pull | Open-drain |
|---|---|---|
| **Circuit** | P-MOS to VCC and N-MOS to GND | One N-MOS to GND |
| **States** | Drives HIGH and LOW | Drives LOW, otherwise floats (high impedance) |
| **Extra parts** | None | Needs a **pull-up** to reach HIGH |
| **Speed** | Fast, symmetrical edges | Slower rise, set by the pull-up and line capacitance (RC) |
| **Use** | LEDs, logic inputs, SPI and UART lines | **Shared buses (I2C)**, level shifting, wired-AND |

- Neither is "better". **Push-pull** is the default for speed and simplicity. **Open-drain** lets **several devices share one wire** without fighting each other.

### 0.4.6 Programming GPIO
- **Bare-metal register access**, such as `PORT_DIRECTION_REGISTER = 0x01;`:
  - **Pros:** maximum speed and zero overhead.
  - **Cons:** not portable, error-prone, needs the hardware manual, and open to **read-modify-write** bugs.
- **HAL (hardware abstraction layer)**, such as FSP's `R_IOPORT_PinWrite(&g_ioport_ctrl, BSP_IO_PORT_01_PIN_00, BSP_IO_LEVEL_HIGH);`:
  - **Pros:** portable across the family, readable and safer.
  - **Cons:** a small overhead, which the compiler usually optimizes away.
- The **FSP pin configurator** in **e² studio** sets each pin's mode, symbolic name, drive capacity and output type (CMOS push-pull or open-drain), then generates the setup code (`pin_data.c`).

### 0.4.7 Design Calculations
- **LED current-limiting resistor:** an LED has almost no resistance, so wired straight to a pin it draws too much current. Use **R = (VOH − VF) / IF**.
  - A **blue LED** (VF ≈ 3.0 V) **can't be driven reliably** from a 3.3 V pin, whose guaranteed VOH(min) is about VCC − 0.5 V = 2.8 V. This is a common design error.
  - A **red LED** (VF ≈ 1.8 V) at 10 mA needs (2.8 − 1.8) / 0.010 = **100 Ω**. The course picks **120 Ω** for margin.
  - Check the worst case: at a full 3.3 V output, 120 Ω gives (3.3 − 1.8) / 120 = **12.5 mA**. That must stay within the pin's **IOH limit for the drive capacity you set**, so check the datasheet.
- **Fan-out** is the number of logic inputs one output can drive: **min(IOH / IIH, IOL / IIL)**.
  - For old **TTL** logic it was about 10.
  - **CMOS** inputs draw only nA to µA, so DC fan-out is huge. The real limit is **capacitive (AC) loading**, which slows the edges.

- Mechanical contacts **bounce** for a few milliseconds when they close or open. A 200 MHz MCU sees this as **dozens of presses**, so one press might count as 17.
- **Debouncing** ignores the noisy transitions and registers **one change per press**.

| Technique | How | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **RC filter** | A resistor and capacitor smooth out the bounces | No CPU time, deterministic | Extra parts, fixed timing |
| **Schmitt trigger** | Hysteresis turns the slow RC edge into a clean one | Excellent noise immunity | More cost than an RC filter alone |
| **Software delay** | Wait about 20 ms after the first edge, then read again | Simple, no hardware | **Blocking**, wastes CPU, can miss events |
| **Software state machine** | Poll the pin and accept a change only after N stable samples | Non-blocking, flexible timing | More code, some CPU time |
| **MCU peripheral** | The Renesas **ICU's digital noise filter** ignores short pulses | Hardware-assisted, low CPU use, configurable | Uses a peripheral, not on every pin |

### 0.4.9 PWM on a GPIO Pin
- A GPIO can only be fully ON or OFF, but **switching it fast** fakes an analog level, which is PWM.
- **Bit-banging** PWM by toggling the pin in a timed loop is inefficient and imprecise. Use the **GPT**, which drives the pin with **zero CPU involvement** once it's configured. That's the right way to dim LEDs, set motor speed or make simple tones.
